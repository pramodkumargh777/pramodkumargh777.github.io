Novelist, Yakshagana reformer, environmentalist, children's encyclopaedist. Called Kadalateerada Bhargava, the sage of the coast. He made Kannada feel big enough to hold everything.

  • Won the Jnanpith Award in 1977 for Mookajjiya Kanasugalu.

Favourite author · b. 1976

Historian · Hebrew University of Jerusalem

He zooms out until humanity fits on a single page, then asks uncomfortable questions about where the page goes next.

  • Sapiens grew out of an undergraduate world-history course he taught.
  • Core idea I keep returning to: shared fictions (money, nations, companies) are how strangers cooperate at scale.
  • Meditates for hours every day and is famously cautious about smartphones.
  • In Nexus, AI is the first technology that makes decisions and generates ideas on its own.
Chapter 3 · Margin notes

Lines I keep, and thoughts of my own

On work

Standards are contagious in both directions

A team doesn't drift into mediocrity in one decision. It drifts one "good enough for now" at a time. The fix is boring: write down what good looks like, and refuse to ship below it even when nobody would notice.

On AI

An LLM is a consensus machine

It predicts the most likely next word, so its default is the average of everything it has read. Markets punish the average view. Useful work with these models comes from feeding them what the average doesn't know: your data, your constraints, your taste.

On money

Compounding is a patience test disguised as a maths problem

The formula is simple. The hard part is sitting through the first ten years, when it looks like nothing is happening, and not interrupting it.

On tennis

You only need to win slightly more than half

The scoring system amplifies small edges. A player who wins 54% of points wins most matches. Careers, portfolios and codebases work the same way. Consistency beats brilliance.

On reading

Read old books to understand new technology

Karanth wrote about villages meeting modernity. Harari writes about species meeting algorithms. It's the same story at different scales, and both make me a better engineer than any framework changelog.

Chapter 6 · On screen

Two stories I rewatch, for opposite reasons

One is about a man who refuses mediocrity and loses himself. The other is about a system that accepted it and lost a city.

AMC · 2008–2013 · 5 seasons · 62 episodes · Albuquerque

Vince Gilligan pitched it as turning Mr. Chips into Scarface. What keeps me coming back is that Walter White's fall is a compounding story. No single choice ruins him. Each small compromise earns a little interest, until the empire and the collapse arrive together.

Mr. ChipsScarface

  • A real chemist, Dr. Donna Nelson, advised the writers so the science would hold up.
  • Bryan Cranston won four lead-actor Emmys for the role.
  • The show was first set in California, then moved to New Mexico for production incentives. Albuquerque became a character.
  • Walt's obsession with purity is my desk quote taken to its darkest extreme. Standards matter. So does what you do with them.
Reactor No. 4 · 26 April 1986

Chernobyl

The HBO series and the documentaries around it are the best incident postmortem I've ever watched. A known design flaw nobody was allowed to talk about, a delayed safety test, and a tired night shift under pressure. Step through the night.

Reactor power

What I took from it

Every incident review I run now starts with one question: why did this look like the reasonable thing to do at the time? Systems fail where people stop being allowed to say what they know.
